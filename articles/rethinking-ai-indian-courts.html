Rethinking AI In Indian Courts

The DPDP Challenge And The Future Of Digital Justice

By Advocate Jayant S

Artificial Intelligence is one of the emerging technologies in the recent times, which has percolated into every discipline across the globe. The judiciary is no exception to this. The contemporary trends of using AI in the decision-making process has given rise to novel challenges such as hallucination, opaqueness, cyber security, and so on. The recent move of the Supreme Court in introducing Regulations for Use of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in Courts, 2026, is indeed a positive move towards achieving the cornerstone vision of Viksit Bharath, 2047.

While the ambitions are decisive, there is a need for caution in the regulatory framework, ensuring inclusive growth and protection of the rights of the individuals; the lack of which will be adversarial to growth. AI in courts must hence be harmonised with the larger privacy framework of India, including the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDP Act), and not merely make a passing mention of it.

While regulating the use of AI is much required, there appears to be incoherence between the DPDP Act and the draft AI Regulation introduced by the Supreme Court. The DPDP Act itself creates a wide exemption for courts and tribunals where processing is necessary for judicial, quasi-judicial, regulatory or supervisory functions.

As such, in so far as AI Regulations refer to the principles outlined under DPDP, this will not be fully applicable to judicial processing cases as most of the DPDP obligations—notice, consent, access, correction, erasure and grievance rights—do not apply in such instances. This leads to a regulatory dissonance as the AI framework seeks data protection rigor, but the overarching privacy regime carves overly sweeping functional exemptions from judicial scrutiny.

Open Courts and Privacy Must Be Balanced Carefully

Difficulties arise in navigating the system and realising the potential violation of the right to privacy. Regulation 10 rightly provides for "Data Protection and Privacy", maintaining that purpose limitation, data minimisation and data privacy shall be governed by the DPDP Act.

However, it is questionable as to who will be the "Data Fiduciary" and the process of enforcement of the duties of such data fiduciary in accordance with the DPDP Act.

According to the Act, a "Data Fiduciary" means any person who alone or in conjunction with other persons determines the purpose and means of processing personal data. In the case of use of AI in the judicial process, there is involvement of multiple stakeholders including advocates, court officers and judges. This leaves room for more complexity in identifying the data fiduciary and the potential data breach. A provision under the Regulations clearly identifying the Data Fiduciary could ideally resolve some of the complexities that may arise.

Contrasting Regulatory Authorities

There appears to be conflict in the provisions regarding the regulatory authority. As far as the aspects falling under the DPDP Act are concerned, the role of Data Protection Officers and the Data Protection Board is critical. Under Section 33 of the DPDP Act, the Board is empowered to impose monetary penalties extending up to ₹250 crore depending upon the severity of the offence.

However, for violations of the Regulations for Use of AI, the AI Committee or the concerned Courts, as the case may be under Regulations 52 and 53, are empowered to decide the remedial measures. Adding to this is Section 39 of the DPDP Act, which imposes a bar of jurisdiction of courts on matters falling under the DPDP Act. This gives rise to challenges in determining the regulatory ambit of the Data Protection Board and the AI Committees or Courts.

DPDP Rights May Be Weakened by Judicial Exemptions

Additionally, the Regulations attempt to highlight the permissible use and prohibited use of AI under Regulations 19 and 20 respectively. Regulation 20 specifically highlights strict prohibition of AI in certain court proceedings, calling it absolute and non-derogable without providing sufficient remedial measures in the event of violations.

Regulation 21 empowers the AI Committee established under the Regulations to direct remedial measures without providing sufficient parameters for determining such violations, while suggesting suspension of the relevant AI system as the upper limit of the remedy. In this context, there appears to be a gap between the intent of the Regulations and the actual legal framework.

Data Collected in Court for Adjudication Should Not Automatically Become Training Data for AI Systems

Demurrals can also be witnessed in the provisions relating to data integrity under Regulation 14, which requires AI systems to be trained to ensure accuracy and avoid bias. Practically, AI models cannot be trained without being fed large-scale information. Algorithmically developed models cannot generate reliable outputs without sufficient reasoning data.

Within the judicial realm, there are myriad social issues existing in multiple dimensions that AI systems need to understand. These include the facts of individual cases, the background of parties involved and the various legislative frameworks applicable to different disputes. Feeding such sensitive information into AI systems has the potential to encroach upon the privacy rights of individuals without their knowledge or informed participation.

Conclusion

While the initiative of the Supreme Court is welcoming to the technologically growing world, it is of utmost importance that such frameworks are drafted with adequate safeguards, ensuring protection for vulnerable sections of society and preserving the fundamental rights guaranteed under the Constitution of India.

The real challenge is not whether courts should use Artificial Intelligence, but whether judicial AI can be made compatible with India's emerging privacy framework. There is a constitutional tension here: the very institution handling some of the most sensitive personal data may operate in a manner that falls outside traditional data protection rights.

Accordingly, the final AI Regulations must not only be DPDP compliant in form but should also establish an independent judicial data protection architecture. Without such safeguards, AI-enabled courts may improve efficiency, but potentially at the cost of privacy, dignity, due process and public trust—values that remain central to the vision of a Viksit Bharat.
