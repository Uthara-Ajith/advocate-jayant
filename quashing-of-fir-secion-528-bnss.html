Quashing of FIR Under Section 528 BNSS

Understanding the High Court's inherent powers, grounds for quashing and the legal procedure.

Introduction

The registration of a First Information Report (FIR) may expose a person to criminal investigation, arrest-related consequences, and prolonged legal proceedings. However, not every criminal case necessarily deserves to proceed to trial. In appropriate circumstances, the High Court has the power to quash an FIR, criminal complaint, charge sheet, or consequential criminal proceedings to prevent abuse of the process of law and secure the ends of justice.

Under the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS), the relevant provision is Section 528, which preserves the inherent powers of the High Court. This provision corresponds to Section 482 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 (CrPC).

Understanding the grounds for quashing criminal proceedings, the circumstances in which this power may be exercised, and the procedure for approaching the High Court is important for anyone facing criminal allegations.

What Is Section 528 of the BNSS?

Section 528 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, deals with the saving of the inherent powers of the High Court. It provides that nothing in the Sanhita shall limit or affect the inherent powers of the High Court to make orders necessary:

  1. To give effect to any order under the BNSS.
  2. To prevent abuse of the process of any court.
  3. Otherwise to secure the ends of justice.

The provision recognises the High Court's inherent jurisdiction to intervene in exceptional cases where allowing criminal proceedings to continue would result in injustice or abuse of the legal process.

The power is extraordinary in nature and must be exercised judicially, based on the facts and circumstances of each case.

Grounds for Quashing an FIR Under Section 528 BNSS

An application for quashing may be considered where the facts and applicable law justify the exercise of the High Court's inherent jurisdiction. Some commonly recognised grounds include the following.

1. The Allegations Do Not Disclose a Criminal Offence

Where the allegations in the FIR or complaint, even if accepted at face value, do not disclose the essential ingredients of any criminal offence, the High Court may consider quashing the proceedings.

A mere allegation of wrongdoing does not automatically establish a criminal offence. The court examines whether the factual allegations, taken as stated, satisfy the legal requirements of the offences invoked.

2. Criminal Proceedings Arising From a Civil Dispute

Certain disputes relating to contracts, financial transactions, property, or business dealings may have both civil and criminal dimensions. In appropriate cases, criminal proceedings may be challenged where the allegations disclose only a civil dispute and fail to establish the essential elements of a criminal offence.

However, the existence of a civil remedy does not, by itself, justify quashing a criminal case. If the allegations independently disclose a criminal offence, criminal proceedings may lawfully continue.

3. Abuse of the Process of Law

The High Court may intervene where the available material demonstrates that criminal proceedings are being used for an improper purpose, such as harassment, retaliation, or exerting unlawful pressure.

The applicant must establish a legally sustainable basis for alleging abuse of process. A mere assertion that the complaint is false or motivated is not necessarily sufficient.

4. Legal Bar to the Continuation of Proceedings

Where a statutory prohibition or other legally recognised bar prevents the institution or continuation of criminal proceedings, the High Court may examine whether its inherent jurisdiction should be exercised.

The nature of the statutory restriction, the applicable procedural provisions, and the facts of the case will determine whether quashing is appropriate.

5. Inherently Improbable Allegations

In exceptional circumstances, allegations may be so inherently improbable or legally untenable that they do not provide a sufficient basis for continuing the prosecution.

The High Court does not ordinarily conduct a full trial while deciding a quashing petition. It generally examines the allegations and relevant material within the limits of its quashing jurisdiction rather than deciding disputed questions of fact that require evidence.

Can an FIR Be Quashed on the Basis of a Compromise?

A settlement between the complainant and the accused may be relevant when considering a petition under Section 528 BNSS. However, a compromise does not automatically result in quashing.

The High Court considers the nature and gravity of the alleged offence, the impact on society, the circumstances of the settlement, and whether quashing would serve the ends of justice.

In appropriate cases involving predominantly private or personal disputes, the court may consider quashing proceedings following a genuine settlement, even where the offence is not compoundable under the applicable statutory provision.

On the other hand, serious offences, offences involving significant public interest, and offences having wider societal consequences are not ordinarily quashed merely because the parties have reached an agreement.

The legal principles governing compromise-based quashing have been explained by the Supreme Court in Parbatbhai Aahir v. State of Gujarat, (2017) 9 SCC 641.

Can the High Court Quash an FIR Before the Investigation Is Completed?

The High Court's inherent jurisdiction is not necessarily unavailable merely because an investigation is at an early stage. Where the legal requirements for intervention are satisfied, the court may consider quashing even before the investigation is completed.

However, the stage of the investigation, the nature of the allegations, the material available, and the need for further factual inquiry are relevant considerations.

The Supreme Court has clarified that there is no absolute rule preventing intervention at the initial stage where the circumstances warrant it. The power must nevertheless be exercised cautiously and in accordance with established legal principles.

Procedure for Filing a Quashing Petition Under Section 528 BNSS

A petition seeking quashing of criminal proceedings generally requires careful examination of the FIR, the alleged offences, the available documents, and the applicable law.

The usual steps include:

Step 1: Examination of the FIR and Allegations

The FIR or complaint is examined to identify the alleged offences, the factual allegations, and the legal ingredients that the prosecution must establish.

Step 2: Review of Supporting Documents

Depending on the case, relevant documents may include the FIR, complaint, statements or other investigation materials where legally appropriate, agreements, communications, settlement documents, and previous court orders.

Step 3: Identification of Legally Sustainable Grounds

The petition must establish why the proceedings warrant interference under Section 528 BNSS. The grounds may relate to the absence of essential ingredients of an offence, abuse of process, a legal bar, or other recognised circumstances.

Step 4: Filing Before the Competent High Court

The petition is presented before the High Court having jurisdiction, in accordance with the applicable procedural and filing requirements. The State and other necessary parties are arrayed as respondents, as appropriate.

Step 5: Hearing and Judicial Consideration

The High Court considers the pleadings, submissions, relevant material, and applicable legal principles before deciding whether to quash the proceedings, decline relief, or pass another appropriate order.

The exact procedure and documents required depend on the nature and stage of the criminal case.

Important Supreme Court Principles on Quashing Criminal Proceedings

The principles laid down in State of Haryana v. Bhajan Lal, 1992 Supp (1) SCC 335, remain an important reference for determining when criminal proceedings may be quashed. The judgment identifies illustrative categories of cases in which intervention may be justified, including allegations that do not disclose an offence, proceedings barred by law, and proceedings manifestly attended by mala fides.

These categories are illustrative rather than exhaustive. The inherent power must be exercised sparingly and with caution.

In R.P. Kapur v. State of Punjab, AIR 1960 SC 866, the Supreme Court also explained the circumstances in which the High Court's inherent jurisdiction may be exercised to quash criminal proceedings.

These decisions continue to inform the exercise of inherent jurisdiction under Section 528 BNSS, subject to the applicable law and procedural framework.

Quashing of Criminal Proceedings Before the Kerala High Court

Individuals facing criminal proceedings in Kerala may, where the legal requirements are satisfied, seek appropriate relief before the High Court of Kerala at Ernakulam.

Whether an FIR or criminal proceeding can be quashed depends on the specific allegations, the offences invoked, the material available, and the applicable judicial principles. A petition must be supported by appropriate legal grounds and relevant documents.

Persons seeking a criminal lawyer in Kochi or a criminal advocate in Ernakulam should obtain advice specific to their case before deciding whether to pursue a quashing petition or another legal remedy.

Conclusion

Section 528 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, preserves the inherent powers of the High Court to prevent abuse of the judicial process and secure the ends of justice. It provides an important legal remedy in appropriate cases where an FIR, complaint, or criminal proceeding should not be permitted to continue.

However, quashing is an exceptional remedy and is not available merely because the accused disputes the allegations or believes that the prosecution will fail. The High Court applies established legal principles and examines the circumstances of each case before granting relief.

A careful legal assessment of the allegations, supporting documents, and applicable precedents is therefore essential when considering a petition for quashing under Section 528 BNSS.
